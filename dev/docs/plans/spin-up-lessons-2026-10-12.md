# Lessons from spinning up LangWatch — 12 October 2026

## One-page summary

Separate stack names do not give them separate capacity. Most stacks and trial cells share one ClickHouse server. They also compete for the Mac's memory, CPU, Postgres connections and disk.

A fixsh restart missed a 25-minute window. One trial spent 45 minutes above load 150 before it was stopped. A fixclean seed was stopped after about 40 minutes. These are separate observations, not a total of time lost. [E1–E4]

Some pressure came from other agents. Some came from the trials themselves. One snapshot carried about two million jobs. After paced seeding and draining, its replacement held 743. Restore fell from 395 seconds to 15 seconds. That proves the value of a smaller backlog; it does not prove the app can drain two million jobs. [E3]

The app has its own cost. Loading backend code took 36.7 of 48.3 seconds in one boot. Three later dev processes used 1.7–2.3 GB each. Loading less code is a strong next target. [E9–E10]

**Do these first:**

1. Verify cleanup and seed fixes in the installed tools. An unread queue must never mean an empty queue. Destroyed stacks must leave no old jobs.
2. Use rush as the one machine gate. Give each timed trial a quiet window. Keep light work moving.
3. Give trials a separate, bounded native ClickHouse. Both servers still share the Mac.
4. Reuse verified snapshots and builds. Use tiny, fresh data for normal UI tests.
5. Set a clear production log level. Make still-mode backend startup load built code.

The larger new work is trial isolation and lighter backend loading. Savings below are estimates unless explicitly measured.

## Scope and evidence

Read-only research covering 28 September–12 October, with most trial evidence from 9–12 October. Inspected 172 comments returned for #8560 and 2,500 for #8553; inventoried 145 cell reports and 111 resource sample files; searched 134 distinct recent transcript file variants after removing duplicate basename/size copies. Searches were targeted, not whole-transcript reads. Also read the project feed, haven/upgradelab docs, relevant memory notes and current source. No stacks, databases, installs, tests or builds were run. No `.env` files were opened.

The source checkout was at `c07b1cb868`, with extensive concurrent changes, including upgradelab changes. “In source” below means present in this working tree, not proven installed or deployed. Issue comments and transcripts are historical observations. Plans in transcripts are not proof of implementation.

Load figures are one-minute load averages unless stated otherwise, not CPU percentages. The historical trial rule treated load above 40 as invalid for timings. That was a screening rule on a 10-core Mac, not evidence of an idle machine. Start/end samples alone miss spikes. Keep correctness results, timing validity and incomplete checks separate.

## What happened: ten pain points and their causes

### 1. ClickHouse capacity was shared even when databases were separate

**Evidence.** Three sweep lanes hit ClickHouse's **100-query limit** and Postgres's connection limit. A later rerun passed the hybrid M checks. No writes were lost in the cited sweep, despite the overload errors. Earlier run22 was recorded as 846 apparently lost writes on a host at load 250–350; that run was void, so it is not evidence of a product data-loss defect. Manual removal of a leftover T5 database is recorded in a transcript. [E5, E12]

**Machine sharing:** one server supplies query slots, memory, merge work and disk I/O to unrelated databases. Separate names isolate data, not resource consumption. Hybrid “private” databases are not separate compute unless the endpoint actually points to another server. The replicated G3 experiment was an exception: it ran two native replicas. [E13]

**Tooling:** haven renders a cap of 100 and a merge pool of two in `tools/thuishaven/domain/clickhouse.go`. Current pressure cleanup runs about every two minutes at 85% tracked memory or 4,000 active parts, but selects tracked, unowned haven databases. Its stray database matcher does not include `upgradelab_*`. Those thresholds are policy estimates, not measured safe limits for this workload. [S1]

**App:** each API, worker and private-target client consumes a share of the same budget. Pool sizing already accepts fleet information; a per-process limit is insufficient when tools cannot count all sibling stacks and cells. Temporary overload also surfaced as HTTP 500 and ERROR logs in the sweeps. [E5, S2]

### 2. Uncoordinated starts destroyed timing windows and live test sessions

**Evidence.** T5-a began at load **89.37**, T5-b at **70.76**; both timings were void. T5-c began at **17.21**, ended at **34.17**, and was counted. Fixsh's Go lane failed to return within **25 minutes** at load **160–226**. Fixlite announced **seven full-service restarts over about 45 minutes**. Another walk ended with the app killed, exit 137, at load **118**; the evidence does not establish who or what sent the kill. [E1–E2, E6]

A particularly clear sampling trap: `t7r3-m/report.md` records start/end loads **27.75/25.81**, but `t7r3-m.load-samples:1` records **150.61** in between. Feed messages asked whether fixclean could wait **20 minutes** while T7 was running. [E7]

**Machine sharing:** boot, seed, typecheck, browser and trial work overlap. A quiet start offers no protection once another stack starts.

**Tooling:** coordination lived in chat. Trial preparation, timed execution and post-run checks were not reliably separated. A long-running experiment needs a declared quiet interval and continuous samples, not another universal load-based command queue.

**App:** fresh code loading and queue catch-up are bursts, so a newly started stack is much more disruptive than an already-idle one. Conversely, t6-c itself drove much of the load: its reporter observed load dropping to 31 after stopping it. Do not blame every spike on other agents. [E3]

### 3. Seeding created work faster than the worker could finish it

**Evidence.** t6-c restored **1,997,313 queued jobs**, peaked at **2,040,262**, and still had **1,670,882** after **45 minutes** of settle. Recipe r3 paced telemetry in waves and drained before capture: **118k → 5 jobs in 30m23s**, then a deliberate trickle left **743** in the snapshot. t6-e restored in **14.579s**, versus **394.588s** for t6-c; its queue peaked at **3,138**, drained in **452.513s**, and ended at **32**. [E3, E8]

**Machine sharing:** backlog processing hit the shared server, extending the slowdown to dev stacks.

**Tooling:** a burst followed by “wait until drained” is not backpressure. The earlier N4 baseline could also accept a huge inherited backlog as normal. The paced producer now exists, but **current `tools/upgradelab/seed/heavy.go:68` still returns `paceGo` when the queue read fails**. This is a current code finding, not a demonstrated cause of the historical T6 failure. It repeats the failure pattern separately fixed in seedgen. [S3]

**App:** one input creates multiple processing jobs and queries. Queue fairness, control operations and recovery throughput matter under backlog. A drained snapshot is the default-upgrade case; a huge backlog remains a separate resilience test.

### 4. “Destroy” did not always mean a clean next run

**Evidence.** Destroying fixclean left Redis DB 4 with **494,819 keys**, including about **120,000 old job groups**. The new stack reused it. Its worker repeatedly hit deleted projects and retried the PII path; the intended seed was not run. The same report records an upgrade lease lost after **80 seconds**, and a service-selection problem that made readiness wait for services switched off. A separate cell hit **EADDRINUSE on port 3300**. [E4, E14]

**Machine sharing:** stale jobs consumed worker/ClickHouse capacity after the original SQL databases were gone. Shared Redis indexes and fixed auxiliary ports need ownership just as SQL databases do.

**Tooling:** store lifecycle was incomplete. **Current source now flushes the destroyed stack's Redis DB**, refusing to flush an index another registered stack uses; tests cover that behavior. Cleanup failure only warns, so reuse still needs a verified-clean decision. Trial processes and databases also need cleanup after cancellation, not only successful completion. [S4]

**App:** the historical deleted-project jobs retried indefinitely, with repeated fallback work. Distinguish an authoritative permanent deletion from a transient lookup failure before making such jobs terminal; do not weaken the PII refusal.

### 5. Nested gates made short checks wait behind long work

**Evidence.** T0's I7 vitest check waited about **25 minutes after settle** while the machine gate wanted load below 10. `t0-rerun/report.md:45` records **1,597,060ms** in checks. A transcript shows vitest waiting at load **20 on 10 cores**, then oxlint waiting at **29–36**. The feed records a typecheck holding a slot for **14 minutes**. [E15]

**Machine sharing:** memory pressure matters, but a host-wide load number cannot tell whether a small check can safely run.

**Tooling:** haven and rush could both wrap a command. Alex's 12 October memory note selects **rush as the one gate**, memory-based admission and age-based CPU priority, with haven passing through. Current haven recognizes `RUSH_GATE_HELD` and rush's Node preload; its hook also stands aside for rush-owned programs. This is partly implemented, not a proposal to introduce a third scheduler. [S5]

**App:** no application defect is needed to explain this wait. Report gate time separately from app startup and upgrade time.

### 6. Preparation was repeated, and skipping it caused different failures

**Evidence.** An October 9 investigation found three serialized `ensure-built` calls per boot. Each paid **5.0s warm or 44.9s cold** graph/hash overhead while the builds themselves took **1.3s**; the reported boot loss was about **2.5 minutes**. A later isolated install took **12.902s**. In the trials, t6-e's build took **105.409s**, while the warm t1-rerun2 build check took **17ms**. These are different runs, not a controlled speedup. [E16, E8]

Skipping install was not a safe fix: a new `@langwatch/share-contract` package was absent in the head checkout and stopped a trial at the switch. The harness subsequently added frozen-lockfile install. [E17]

**Machine sharing:** repeated builds consume the same cores and memory, and can invalidate somebody else's trial.

**Tooling:** `Build.Fresh` in upgradelab checks checkout HEAD plus one output path. A changed HEAD reruns preparation even for unchanged build inputs; dirty source changes can instead be missed under the same HEAD. The head script also requests **16 TypeScript builders** on a 10-core host. Shared Nx caching already exists, and ADR-150 retired the older custom `ensure-built` cache. Do not resurrect that obsolete fix. [S6]

**App:** the dependency/build graph is substantial, but no reliable total “minutes lost to pnpm install” was recovered. Preserve correct workspace links and generated assets while sharing immutable outputs.

### 7. A dev stack loads a large backend before it can serve

**Evidence.** The fixdrive boot study measured **219s launcher-to-ready** on the initial launch. A later current-ledger cold generation took **48.345s**: **36.740s loading/linking code**, **11.605s runtime boot**; the ledger check was only **70ms**. The original first-install upgrade subprocess took **43.076s**, not the entire readiness wait. [E9]

Three processes measured with `vmmap` later held **1.7, 2.1 and 2.3 GB physical footprint**, with **2.9–3.3 GB boot peaks** and **42–43 threads**. The current API and worker composition lists each statically import **63 process modules**. There are **64 process-package manifests** across core and Enterprise, plus **44 browser-package manifests**; these counts describe source organization, not 108 operating-system processes. [E10, S7]

**Machine sharing:** several roughly 2 GB stacks plus browser pages and builds leave little room for startup peaks. Historical browser measurements found **1.8–2.9 GB with six pages**. [E10]

**Tooling:** default still mode already serves built UI, but its backend still uses Vite's module runner. Source, transformed code, source maps and compiled code remain resident. Inline map duplication has already been removed: a backend import comparison reduced post-GC heap **1,108 → 503–506 MB**. Do not count that as future savings. [S8]

**App:** API and worker build separate containers even inside one Node process. Large imports include the generated Vega validator, Prisma and query parsers. The older boot trace showed a **7–9s** voice-tunnel opportunity; the current voice service now acquires the tunnel on first voice use. Starting the 57 outbox workers took **9ms**, so their count is not evidence of a slow startup barrier. [E9, S7–S8]

### 8. Query and background-work amplification made backlog recovery expensive

**Evidence.** In the T6 comparison, removing `FINAL` from metric roll-up reads reduced measured maximum per-read memory from **1.54 GiB to 0.74 GiB**, and in-flight query memory from **6.69 to 1.77 GiB**. Still, t6-c recorded **1,976 cold scans of `trace_summaries` in four minutes**, roughly **8/s**. Its 34 trace-meter commands waited behind about **830k older groups**; operator access did not become usable within the 45-minute observation. [E3]

**Machine sharing:** expensive reads compete with writes and background merges on the same server.

**Tooling:** small-backlog success can hide this problem. Keep the heavy-backlog workload, but run it intentionally with resource measurements and a stop condition.

**App:** repeated lookup scans, costly merge-time read semantics, and control work sharing long data queues all amplify the workload. Existing client read/insert limits help, but need a host-wide allocation. Prioritize query plans and batching in measured callers; do not remove `FINAL` indiscriminately or substitute trace summaries for analytics data. Index work already has a separate plan, `id-index-2026-10-10.md`; coordinate with that work.

### 9. Debug logging added substantial work and obscured failures

**Evidence.** Main's worker wrote **750 MB in 22 minutes**, about **34 MB/minute**, during snapshot production. Both releases defaulted to debug without an explicit level, even with production mode. Three recovered ClickHouse insert timeouts then failed the log invariant. Another comparison found **140 ERROR lines at shutdown** because Redis closed before the worker. [E3, E18]

**Machine sharing:** writing, formatting, collecting and scanning these logs competes with database I/O and startup. There is no controlled measurement of its share of total CPU.

**Tooling:** record the log policy in the run manifest. Changing it between releases or comparisons confounds results. Shutdown ordering is part of producing trustworthy evidence.

**App:** `packages/observability/src/logger-config.ts:56` still sets default level `debug`; non-test normalization uses that default. Console and OTLP thresholds are distinct. Recovered retries should be recognizable without looking like terminal failures, while exhausted retries and lost writes must stay visible. [S9]

### 10. “Small” test data was too large for ordinary UI work

**Evidence.** fixclean's small/all-persona seed sent **50k spans, 50k logs and 100k metric points** in **2m53s**. Reported processing was about **65 spans/s**, against **289 sent/s**. After ten minutes, **173,437 jobs were due**. It also had **22 plan-limit refusals** and **687 dependent reference failures**. [E19]

A later run eliminated those refusals but reached **247,447 jobs**, and was stopped after **about 40 minutes**. Nine unread backlog checks falsely resumed seeding. Current seedgen now keeps a paused guard paused when its signal is unread and will not grow its send window without a backlog reading. Medium/large estimates of **1h32m/10h08m** were dry-run estimates, not observed runtimes. [E4, E19, S3]

**Machine sharing:** seeding is sustained ingestion, not cheap fixture setup.

**Tooling:** size, persona coverage, plan entitlement and queue amplification were mixed together. Fresh, minimal fixtures should be the default; stress volume should be a separate choice.

**App:** oversized accumulated membership data also changes normal page cost. A 30 September memory records `organization.getAll` taking **about 43s** for an admin in **8 orgs, 405 teams and 167 projects** accumulated by test runs. This is secondary evidence, not a new query benchmark. Reset data first, then profile representative product workloads. [E20]

### Boundaries that keep these changes useful

- **Isolation is not more capacity.** Start with one trial ClickHouse plus the dev server, not a server per test. Keep shared Postgres within an aggregate connection budget. Upgradelab already starts a private Redis process per cell; haven dev stacks use indexes on a shared Redis.
- **Cleanup needs ownership.** Drop only a completed/expired run's stores after its writers stop. Do not kill another stack's queries or cancel mutations globally. Do not routinely run `OPTIMIZE FINAL` to “clear” a backlog; that adds work and changes the measured state.
- **Scheduling is not another shell queue.** Retain Alex's single-gate decision. A timed experiment's quiet window is an explicit reservation, not a load threshold that blocks every small command. If the window is broken, mark affected timings invalid automatically. Do not silently raise `-max-load` until a run becomes admissible.
- **Smaller stacks still need their dependencies.** Turning off paymentsim on SaaS made the worker retry every 30s, left **487 steps outstanding**, and kept auto-seed waiting. Use validated service profiles, not arbitrary service removal. [E21]
- **Templates must stay representative.** Capture all participating stores consistently, retain intentional delayed jobs, and compare fingerprints before boot. Preserve the existing synthetic-data scrub without including generated credentials in reports. Measure clone/restore cost before choosing a native-copy shortcut.
- **Do not optimize the wrong numbers.** `resources.jsonl`'s ClickHouse `bytes` value is store size, not server RSS. Several head entries around 75 MB are wrappers, not reliable whole-app memory. The current sampler sums process groups; independently detached descendants still need checking. RSS and macOS physical footprint are different measures. [E10, S10]

## Implementation order and acceptance

**First: operational reliability, without changing product behavior.** Verify the installed haven/rush versions against current source. Prove Redis cleanup and seedgen's unread-signal handling. Close the upgradelab pacing gap. Use tiny fixtures, shared cached builds and recorded log settings. Split prepare, timed trial and post-check phases. Mark the work complete only after a cancelled run leaves no unowned writers and a replacement starts clean.

**Next: isolate and reuse.** Add the single bounded trial ClickHouse and ownership-based cleanup. Produce one verified S snapshot and one normal M snapshot; reuse each across cells. Run three consecutive trials with complete load/pressure samples and no gate wait folded into upgrade timing. Compare time to a usable result, not merely time until the launcher returns.

**Then: reduce app cost.** Benchmark identical code/data/log settings on a quiet host: still-mode backend before/after, three cold boots and three warm boots each. Separate build, code loading, composition, migration, readiness and seed drain. Record full process-tree memory at boot peak and steady state, and after repeated reloads. Aim to remove at least 15 seconds from code loading before considering a wider composition rewrite. Confirm browser, background-job and voice behavior remain intact.

**Keep a separate heavy-data test.** The two-million-job case must not disappear just because everyday fixtures get smaller. Give it a stated queue volume, bounded ingestion rate, maximum duration, and progress criteria. If it cannot make progress within the Mac's budget, move that trial to a dedicated runner; do not turn routine UI testing into a stress test.
